## 1. NASA Official History Sources

### NASA History Office, "60 Years Ago: NASA Selects Its Third Group of Astronauts" (2023)
Source: https://www.nasa.gov/history/60-years-ago-nasa-selects-its-third-group-of-astronauts/

> "On June 5, 1963, NASA announced that it would be recruiting another ten to fifteen new astronauts."

> "Most of the criteria were unchanged from the previous selection: candidates had to be U.S. citizens, 6 feet or less in height, with a degree in engineering or the physical or biological sciences, and recommended by their employer."

> "The test-pilot qualification, while still preferred, was no longer a prerequisite, with 1,000 hours of jet-aircraft pilot time accepted as a substitute, and the maximum age was lowered by one year, so candidates had to be aged less than 34 on June 30, 1963."

> "By the final July 15 deadline, 720 applications were received, of which 492 were from military personnel and 228 were from civilians."

> "Of these, 490 were considered eligible, and 136 were selected for further screening."

> "The selection panel considered the applications between July 17 and 20, and picked the top 34."

> "The 34 selected for further examination were sent to Brooks Air Force Base for medical examinations between July 31 and August 15."

> "A selection panel was established, consisting of Mercury Seven astronauts Deke Slayton, Alan Shepard, Wally Schirra and John Glenn, and NASA test pilot Warren J. North, the chief of Flight Crew Operations."

> "From the 720 applications received by the July deadline, the selection board chose 136 candidates for further screening and narrowed that field down to 34 for extensive medical evaluations at Brooks Air Force Base (AFB) in San Antonio between July 31 and Aug. 15."

> "Chosen from 720 military and civilian applicants, the newest group of 14 astronauts comprised the best educated class up to that time."

### NASA SP-4214, "Where No Man Has Gone Before," Ch. 5-7: "Selection of the First Scientist-Astronauts"

## 2. Wikipedia / Grokipedia (Compiled Encyclopedia Sources — Cite Underlying Refs)

### Wikipedia, "NASA Astronaut Group 3"
Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NASA_Astronaut_Group_3

> "Selection criteria at the time for the candidates included U.S. citizenship, a degree in engineering or physical science, test pilot experience or 1,000 hours flying jets, 34 years old or younger, and no taller than six feet."

> "All were test pilots except Aldrin, Anders, Cernan, Chaffee, Cunningham and Schweickart."

> "Slayton had developed a points system for assessing the candidates. Ten points each were allocated to academics, pilot performance, and character and motivation, for a possible score of thirty points."

> "Slayton took thirteen names to a meeting chaired by the MSC director, Robert R. Gilruth. Maxime Faget, the director of engineering and development, had an objection: thirteen was an unlucky number."

> "The final stage of the selection process were interviews by the selection panel, which were conducted at the Manned Spacecraft Center (MSC) in Houston between September 2 and 7."

### Wikipedia, "NASA Astronaut Group 5" (1966 — parallel test battery, same location)
Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NASA_Astronaut_Group_5

> "Psychological tests included Rorschach tests; physical tests included encephalograms, and sessions on treadmills and a centrifuge."

> "Other tests included some that Lind thought had been originated by the Inquisition, such as plunging a hand into hot water and having cold water poured into the ears."

[NOTE: Group 5 medical evaluations also conducted at Brooks AFB, San Antonio. The Wikipedia article states "the criteria were much the same as those for NASA Astronaut Group 3 in 1963, except that the age requirement was raised from 34 to 36 years of age." This makes Group 5 test descriptions a reasonable proxy for Group 3 procedures.]

### Grokipedia, "NASA Astronaut Group 3"
Source: https://grokipedia.com/page/NASA_Astronaut_Group_3

> "The medical exams incorporated centrifuge simulations to gauge g-force endurance, pressure suit familiarization for vacuum exposure readiness, and isolation protocols to probe psychological resilience against prolonged confinement."

## 3. Specific Medical Tests — Cross-Group Documentation

### National Space Academy, "Project Apollo: Could You Pass the Test?"
Source: https://nationalspaceacademy.org/about-us/news/project-apollo-could-you-pass-the-test/

> "Three of the tests used for the Apollo 11 astronauts are commonly used in fitness and physiological tests today: the Harvard Step test, the Treadmill test and the Cold Pressor test."

> "They didn't tell astronauts about the cold pressor test so it was a surprise!"

### Space Safety Magazine, "Historical Evolution of Astronaut Selection"
Source: https://www.spacesafetymagazine.com/space-exploration/astronaut-cosmonaut/historical-evolution-astronaut-selection/

> "In 1963, for the third recruiting effort, flight requirements were lowered to 1000 hours, non-test pilots were qualified, and the age limit was lowered to 34."

### Wikipedia, "NASA Astronaut Group 3" (Medical Test Details)
Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NASA_Astronaut_Group_3

> "Tests included cardiograms, running on treadmills, electrocephalograms, and blood sugar tests."

> "Hypoxia tests were carried out to see how they reacted to a loss of oxygen, they were spun about in darkened rooms to test their resistance to motion sickness, and ice water was poured into one ear to test how the inner ears reacted to such an imbalance."

### Lovelace Foundation Testing (Mercury-era baseline — same general battery adapted for later groups)

From multiple compiled sources on the Lovelace/Mercury examination:

> "Candidates were subjected to X-ray examinations, detailed eye examinations, analysis of ear, nose and throat function, radiation level testing, total body water tests, bicycle ergometer tests to measure cardiovascular fitness and specific gravity tests."

> "Heart function was scrutinised in every detail imaginable by cardiac specialists."

> "The tests ranged from typical X-rays and general body physicals to the atypical; for instance, candidates had to swallow a rubber tube in order to test the level of their stomach acids."

> "Doctors tested the reflexes in the ulnar nerve of the forearms by using electric shock."

> "Ice water shot into ears to freeze the inner ear while measuring recovery time."

> "Exhaustion tests on specially weighted stationary bicycles to test respiration."

Source: National Space Academy article; Mercury 13 documentation; Lovelace Clinic historical records compiled via Wikipedia "Mercury 13" and "William Randolph Lovelace II" articles.

### Lt. Col. William K. Douglas, USAF, MC, "Preparation of the Astronaut" (1959)
Source: https://spacemedicineassociation.org/download/space_medicine_classics/Preparation.pdf

> "A physical examination conducted by three separate examiners: a Flight Surgeon performed a general physical examination, an internist conducted an extensive examination with special emphasis on the cardiovascular system, and a psychiatrist administered several short psychomotor tests and conducted a brief interview."

[NOTE: This describes Mercury-era preflight examinations but establishes the three-examiner structure that carried forward.]

## 4. Psychological / Psychiatric Selection

### NASA NTRS, "Forty Years of Psychological and Psychiatric Selection of NASA Astronauts" (2000)
Source: https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20000085964

> "Between 1959 and 1987, the psychiatric evaluation of astronaut candidates evolved from a 30-h intensive examination evaluating applicants for psychopathology and studying their performance under stress, to a 2-h clinical interview whose structure and contents were determined by the individual examiner."

### Kelley J. Slack et al., "Selecting Astronauts: The Role of Psychologists," NASA JSC (2014)
Source: https://ntrs.nasa.gov/archive/nasa/casi.ntrs.nasa.gov/20140006498.pdf

> "NASA underutilized, downplayed, then ultimately ignored psychiatric and psychological characteristics in selecting astronauts, until very recently."

> "Early US programmes (Mercury, Gemini, Apollo) recruited almost exclusively from high-performance military aviators."

> "Psychological tests used in the selection process for the space program focused primarily on the detection of gross psychopathologies in potential candidates."

### Space.com, "The Right (Mental) Stuff: NASA Astronaut Psychology Revealed"
Source: https://www.space.com/26799-nasa-astronauts-psychological-evaluation.html

> "Psychological requirements were established for astronauts during the Mercury program, dating back to 1958."

> "Psychiatric evaluation techniques changed from independent reviews with no standardization to use of standard psychological tests identifying history or presence of significant psychiatric disorders, personality disorders, and traits, characteristics, or behaviors that could impact safety of the mission or crew."

### General Psychological Testing (compiled from Group 3/5 Wikipedia sources)

> "A total of 13 psychological tests were used to examine motivation and personality along with dozens of different aptitude tests."

> "Questions like 'Who are you?' — required each candidate to give 20 definitions of themselves."

> "Candidates were given a blank sheet of white paper and asked what it depicted."

[NOTE: The blank-paper test is likely the same one Pete Conrad famously responded to with "It's upside-down" during Mercury selection — see DoctorZebra source below.]

## 5. Astronaut Memoirs and Oral Histories

### R. Walter Cunningham, NASA JSC Oral History, interviewed by Ron Stone, 24 May 1999
Source: https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2024/11/rwc-5-24-99.pdf

On the competition and screening process:
> "They'd started off with about 770 qualified applicants, and I was getting screened without actually having any contact with them and making the cut. And I'd get a call every so often saying, 'You've made the next cut,' you know. 'Do you still want to do this?'"

On being sent to Brooks AFB:
> "When I finally found out who I was competing with, there were 32 of us left. And we went down to the Air Force Medical Center over in San Antonio [Texas], and they were phasing us in there."

On the medical examinations:
> "And I remember stopping and thinking, 'Hey, Walt, this is not going to be easy to get in here.' I mean, I was tremendously impressed with the competition I had."

On being a non-test-pilot:
> "Some of these guys had been flying — half of them were test pilots. I had never gotten to Test Pilot School. They'd been flying later-model airplanes. I'd been in the Reserves. The Regulars all on active duty kind of looked down their nose at the Reserves. They could've looked down their nose at me because I was studying physics instead of being an engineer."

On physical preparation:
> "The only thing that I can ever recall doing specifically to become an astronaut was: the summer of 1963, after applying, I began to work more seriously on my physical condition. I've always been a athlete, a gymnast and did a variety of things, did a lot of swimming. But I recall that summer, for the first time in my life, I began running on the beach in Santa Monica because I read that that's what the astronauts did."

On the elimination process:
> "Well, when we — after the physicals, there were four people [who] were eliminated at the physicals. There were 28 of us left in the running. We didn't know how many they were going to select — yet."

On final interviews:
> "And a couple of weeks later, we showed up for final interviews (and this was in person, 3 or 4 days). And I got to see some of the people that I had not seen during the physical selection. They were even more impressive! And they all seemed to know these guys personally, you know."

On the selection call:
> "And eventually, I got the call from Deke. And he said, 'Well, Walt, we'd like to have you if you still want to go to work for us.'"

### Michael Collins, "Carrying the Fire: An Astronaut's Journeys" (1974)
Source: Collins, Michael. *Carrying the Fire*. New York: Ballantine, 1974.

[NOTE: Book text not digitally accessible via web search. Secondary sources confirm Collins describes the medical evaluation process with humor, "recounting his experiences being probed and prodded not with the serious earnestness of say, John Young, but like a guy at the bar telling a story to amuse his buddies" (Reading Freely book review, 2023). Collins was also noted as having attended an Air Force "prep school" to coach candidates going to be interviewed by NASA.]

### Buzz Aldrin — Selection Background
Sources: Wikipedia "Buzz Aldrin"; Smithsonian NASM "Buzz Aldrin's Doctoral Thesis"

> "Aldrin chose his doctoral thesis in the hope that it would help him be selected as an astronaut, although it meant foregoing test pilot training, which was a prerequisite at the time."

> "His initial application was rejected on the grounds that he was not a test pilot, and after his request for a waiver was turned down, NASA announced another round of selections in May 1963 with the requirement that applicants had either test pilot experience or 1,000 hours of flying time in jet aircraft."

> "In January 1963, Aldrin earned a degree of Doctor of Science from MIT for his thesis 'Line-of-Sight Guidance Techniques for Manned Orbital Rendezvous.'"

> "Aldrin had over 2,500 hours of flying time, of which 2,200 was in jets."

> "His selection as one of fourteen members of NASA's Astronaut Group 3 was announced on October 18, 1963, making him the first astronaut with a doctoral degree."

### DoctorZebra, "Astronaut Selection Hints"
Source: https://doctorzebra.com/drz/s_selection

On Pete Conrad's blank-card incident (Mercury era, but illustrative of the psych tests carried forward):
> "Pete Conrad found out the hard way that it did not pay to joke around on the psychological tests. In the 1959 selection screening he was shown a white card and asked what he saw. He puzzled over it for a time, then said in slow surprise 'It's upside-down.' The examiner was in the midst of getting up to check before he realized Conrad was joking."

Pre-Eisenhower desiderata (from NASA SP-4201, "This New Ocean," p.130):
> "An applicant also must prove: (a) willingness to accept hazards comparable to those encountered in modern research airplane flight; (b) capacity to tolerate rigorous and severe environmental conditions; and (c) ability to react adequately under conditions of stress or emergency."

> "When aeromedical psychologists were requested to recommend the main attributes for an astronaut, they replied the ideal might be a priest who knew how to fly."

## 7. What Changed from Groups 1-2 to Group 3

| Criterion | Mercury Seven (1959) | New Nine (1962) | The Fourteen (1963) |
|-----------|---------------------|-----------------|---------------------|
| Test pilot required | Yes | Yes | No — 1,000 hrs jet time accepted |
| Max age | 39 (at time of selection) | 35 | 34 (by June 30, 1963) |
| Max height | 5'11" | 6'0" | 6'0" |
| Education | Bachelor's in engineering | Bachelor's in eng. or science | Bachelor's in eng. or physical/biological science |
| Military required | Yes (by Eisenhower directive) | No (civilians eligible) | No |
| Applications received | 508 (pre-screened from military records) | 253 | 720 |
| Medical eval location | Lovelace Clinic, Albuquerque + Wright-Patterson, Dayton | Brooks AFB, San Antonio | Brooks AFB, San Antonio |

Source: Compiled from NASA History Office articles on all three selections.

## IMPORTANT CAVEATS

1. **Book-length primary sources not yet extracted.** The most detailed accounts of Group 3 medical/psychological testing are in:
   - Michael Collins, *Carrying the Fire* (1974) — Chapter 3-4 area
   - Walter Cunningham, *The All-American Boys* (1977)
   - Gene Cernan, *The Last Man on the Moon* (1999)
   - Buzz Aldrin, *Return to Earth* (1973) / *Magnificent Desolation* (2009)
   - Donald K. Slayton, *Deke!* (1994)
   - Patricia A. Santy, *Choosing the Right Stuff* (1994) — definitive psychological selection history
   - Colin Burgess and Doolan, Kate — Springer series on astronaut groups

2. **Group 3 vs. Mercury/Group 5 medical tests.** Multiple sources confirm the Brooks AFB medical battery was substantially similar across Groups 2-5 (1962-1966). The specific tests named (treadmill, cold pressor, Harvard step, encephalogram, centrifuge, ice-water caloric test, Rorschach, isolation) are documented for Groups 1 and 5 explicitly; Group 3 sources confirm "medical examinations" at Brooks without always itemizing each test. The Wikipedia article on Group 3 does specifically name cardiograms, treadmills, electroencephalograms, blood sugar tests, hypoxia tests, motion sickness testing, and ice-water ear tests.

3. **Quotes marked as from Wikipedia/Grokipedia** should be traced to their underlying cited references (typically Burgess books, Slayton autobiography, NASA SP-4407) for final publication.
