# NASA Group 1 (Mercury Seven) Selection Criteria: Direct Quotes

## Overview

The Mercury Seven were selected in April 1959 through the most extensively documented astronaut selection process in NASA history. Selection involved four phases: records screening, technical interviews in Washington, medical examinations at the Lovelace Foundation in Albuquerque, and stress/psychological testing at the Aeromedical Laboratory of Wright Air Development Center (Wright-Patterson AFB). NASA Release 59-113 (already documented elsewhere) covered the public announcement. This file collects direct quotes from sources BEYOND that release.

---

## 1. NASA Official History -- "Project Mercury Overview: Astronaut Selection"

**Source:** NASA History Division, "Project Mercury Overview -- Astronaut Selection."
**URL:** https://www.nasa.gov/history/project-mercury-overview-astronaut-selection/

On the Phase III medical examinations at Lovelace:

> "Each candidate arrived at Albuquerque to undergo approximately a week of medical evaluations under each of five different schedules."

On Phase IV at Wright-Patterson:

> "Phase four of the selection program was an amazingly elaborate set of environmental studies, physical endurance tests, anthropometric measurements, and psychiatric studies conducted at the Aeromedical Laboratory of the Wright Air Development Center."

> "While the purpose of the medical examinations at Lovelace Clinic had been to determine the general health status of the candidates, the purpose of the testing program at Wright Field was to determine the physical and psychological capability of the individual to respond effectively and appropriately to the various types of stresses associated with space missions."

On the specific categories of stress tests:

> "In addition to pressure suit tests, acceleration tests, vibration tests, heat tests, and loud noise tests, each candidate had to prove his physical endurance on treadmills, tilt tables, with his feet in ice water, and by blowing up balloons until exhausted."

On psychological testing:

> "Continuous psychiatric interviews, the necessity of living with two psychologists throughout the week, and extensive self-examination through a battery of 13 psychological tests for personality and motivation, and another dozen different tests on intellectual functions and special aptitudes -- these were all part of the week of truth at Dayton."

## 2. NASA History -- "65 Years Ago: NASA Selects America's First Astronauts" (2024)

**Source:** John Uri, "65 Years Ago: NASA Selects America's First Astronauts," NASA History, April 4, 2024.
**URL:** https://www.nasa.gov/history/65-years-ago-nasa-selects-americas-first-astronauts/

On the Lovelace Clinic timeline:

> "Beginning on Feb. 7, the candidates in six groups of five or six spent one week at Lovelace undergoing comprehensive medical examinations."

On advancement from Lovelace to Wright-Patterson:

> "31 of the 32 (one candidate failed a blood test at Lovelace) advanced to the Aero Medical Laboratory (AML) at Wright-Patterson Air Force Base in Dayton, Ohio, where weeklong testing of the six groups took place between Feb. 15 and March 28."

On specific stress test parameters:

> "the candidates were forced to spend an hour in a pressure chamber that simulated an altitude of 65,000 feet, and two hours in a chamber that was heated to 130 degrees Fahrenheit."

On other stress test types:

> "They underwent more testing, which included acceleration tests, vibration tests, heat tests, and loud noise tests, and were required to be placed in centrifuges and altitude chambers to assess their responses."

On the final outcome:

> "Only one candidate, Jim Lovell, was eliminated on medical grounds at this stage; thirteen others were recommended with reservations."

## 9. George Ruff and Edwin Levy -- Psychiatric Evaluation

**Source:** George E. Ruff obituary, *Philadelphia Inquirer*, October 9, 2017; George Ruff entry, University of Pennsylvania Almanac.
**URLs:**
- https://www.inquirer.com/philly/obituaries/dr-george-e-ruff-89-psychiatrist-who-helped-pick-mercury-seven-astronauts-20171009.html
- https://almanac.upenn.edu/articles/george-ruff-psychiatry

On Ruff's role:

> "Ruff was on active Air Force duty from 1957-1959, working in the Aerospace Medical Research Laboratory as chief investigator of the stress and fatigue section at Wright-Patterson Air Force Base in Dayton, Ohio, where he helped select NASA's first group of astronauts to go to space by conducting the psychiatric evaluation for the Mercury Project along with colleague Edwin Z. Levy."

**Source:** Matthew H. Hersch, "Project Astronaut," in *Inventing the American Astronaut* (New York: Palgrave Macmillan, 2012). Chapter available at Springer:
**URL:** https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1057/9781137025296_2

On the psychiatric battery used:

> "Ruff and Ed Levy also conducted conventional psychiatric examinations on the 32 finalists at Lovelace, aided by two psychologists enlisted to observe the applicants during testing, including a battery of tests popular in the late-1950s, including the Rorschach Inkblot Tests and the Minnesota Multiphasic Personality Inventory, as well as a variety of standardized military aptitude exams for officer candidates and aviators, and stress tests to examine cognitive function under duress -- isolation, noise, and other discomforts."

On the interviews in Washington:

> "The interviews in Washington, D.C. included several personality assessments conducted by two Air Force psychiatrists, George Ruff and Ed Levy, who questioned applicants about their personal histories and challenged each to 'sell' himself to his examiners."

On their findings:

> "Ruff and Levy found the group to be entirely free of 'psychosis, clinically significant neurosis or personality disorder'; with the exception of a few applicants whose intellectual aptitude fell below minimum standards, no pilots were struck from the selection process on psychological grounds."

On NASA's restrictions on psychiatric evaluation:

> "NASA insisted that psychiatric examiners only 'screen-out' mental illness in applicants, and were unsympathetic to their proposals to identify persons with qualities that might render them particularly well-suited to spaceflight careers."

## 10. Vintage Space -- "The Right Stuff at Heart" (Amy Shira Teitel, 2011)

**Source:** Amy Shira Teitel, "The Right Stuff at Heart," Vintage Space (blog), July 27, 2011.
**URL:** https://vintagespace.wordpress.com/2011/07/27/the-right-stuff-at-heart/

On the seven cardiovascular tests:

> "the Flack test had astronauts expel breath to keep a ball on top of a column of Mercury at a certain height as long as possible"

> "the Valsalva overshoot test duplicated the Flack test with the addition of blood pressure measurements"

> "the Partial Pressure Suit test put the astronaut through psychological and cardiovascular tests in a simulated high atmosphere environment"

> "the Tilt Table test measured candidates' cardiovascular recovery after exercise on an angled platform"

> "In addition to these four tests were three simpler tests: the Harvard Step test, the Cold Pressor test, and the Treadmill test, all of which are easily self-administered."

On the Cold Pressor Test procedure:

> "Three resting blood pressures and pulses were taken at 1-minute intervals while the subject was relaxed, then he was directed to plunge both feet into a pan of ice water for 7-minutes."

On the Cold Pressor Test setup:

> "The Cold Pressor Test requires a bucket of ice water chilled to 4 degrees centigrade or 39.2 Fahrenheit and a towel."

On the Harvard Step Test:

> "The subject steps 20 inches to a platform once every two seconds for five minutes."

On group performance:

> "The average score of candidates was 52.8 with a standard deviation of 5.3."

## 11. Vintage Space -- "Designing the Perfect Astronaut" (Amy Shira Teitel, 2010)

**Source:** Amy Shira Teitel, "Designing the Perfect Astronaut," Vintage Space (blog), December 7, 2010.
**URL:** https://vintagespace.wordpress.com/2010/12/07/designing-the-perfect-astronaut/

On the psychological testing scope:

> "The testing took roughly 30 hours per candidate and was designed to determine not only which men could withstand the psychological stresses and pressures anticipated from spaceflight, but also why these men wanted to go into space in the first place."

On the three areas profiled:

> "The tests were designed to profile the candidates in three main areas: personality and motivation, intellectual function and special aptitudes, and reactions to stress tests."

On the Rorschach and Thematic Apperception Tests:

> "As part of the Rorschach and Thematic Apperception tests, both designed to analyze personality and motivations, the candidates were asked to describe in detail the story they saw in a given abstract image."

On candidate subversion of the testing:

> "Candidates routinely broke the 'no communication about the tests' rule, speculated on what the 'right' answers to the inkblot cards were, discussed what the psychologists were most likely looking for, and as a form of rebellion, many began giving deliberately misleading or pre-planned answers to the doctors' questions."

On the ideal candidate:

> "The perfect astronaut was one who possessed enthusiasm and willingness without recklessness."

## 16. NASA History -- Lovelace's Woman in Space Program (applied same tests)

**Source:** NASA History Division, "Lovelace's Woman in Space Program."
**URL:** https://www.nasa.gov/history/lovelaces-woman-in-space-program/

The same Lovelace battery was administered to the Mercury 13 women candidates. This NASA page enumerates specific procedures:

> "The Lovelace doctors required very thorough examinations, which included numerous X-rays and a four-hour eye exam."

> "A specially weighted stationary bicycle pushed the women to exhaustion while testing their respiration."

> "The doctors had the women swallow a rubber tube so that they could test their stomach acids."

> "A tilt table tested circulation."

> "Using an electrical pulse, the physicians tested nerve reflexes in their arms."

> "Ice water was shot into the women's ears to induce vertigo so that the doctors could time how quickly they recovered."

> "They calculated the candidates' lean body mass using a nuclear counter in Los Alamos."

**Context note:** These procedures were the same battery Dr. Lovelace had developed for the male Mercury candidates. The NASA page states the women "underwent the same medical testing as NASA's trainee astronauts."

## 25. "Who Am I?" Test

**Source:** Referenced across multiple secondary sources (Vintage Space, Space Safety Magazine, NASA SP-4201).

On the psychological self-identification exercise:

> "The Mercury astronauts were required to answer the test question 'Who am I?' by writing down 20 definitional identifications of themselves, ranked in order of significance, which was interpreted projectively to elicit information on identity and perception of social roles."

**Note:** This is the Twenty Statements Test (TST), developed by Kuhn and McPartland (1954). Its use in Mercury selection is documented but the exact format of the answers given by the seven selectees is not publicly available.

## Composite Test Inventory (compiled from all sources above)

### Phase II -- Washington, D.C. (Technical interviews, January-February 1959)

- Written technical examinations
- Technical interviews with NASA selection committee
- Psychiatric interviews (Ruff and Levy)
- Medical history review
- Miller Analogies Test (IQ measure, administered by Voas)
- Minnesota Engineering Analogies Test (engineering aptitude, administered by Voas)
- Doppelt Mathematical Reasoning Test (mathematical aptitude, administered by Voas)

### Phase III -- Lovelace Foundation, Albuquerque, NM (Medical, ~1 week per group)

**Laboratory tests (over 30):**
- Blood draws and blood chemistry panels
- Urine samples
- Stool samples
- Throat scrapings
- Stomach contents measurement (rubber tube swallowed)
- Barium enemas ("diagnostic enemas"; five total per candidate per AmericaSpace)
- Castor oil doses (three per candidate per AmericaSpace)
- Total body volume (submersion in water tanks)
- Electrocardiogram (EKG/ECG)
- Electroencephalogram (EEG)
- Liver dye injection
- Prostate examination

**Imaging:**
- Comprehensive X-ray examinations ("thoroughly mapped their body")

**Ophthalmology:**
- Four-hour eye examination
- Lights shone into eyes

**Otolaryngology (ENT):**
- Ear, nose, and throat examinations
- Cold water/ice water injected into ears (caloric stimulation to induce vertigo; recovery timed)
- Sinus probing

**Cardiovascular/Physiological:**
- Heart and pulse rate measurements
- Blood pressure measurements
- Bicycle ergometer test (to exhaustion, measuring cardiovascular fitness and respiration)
- Tilt table test (circulation)

**Neuromuscular:**
- Brain wave measurement
- Muscular reactions to electric current (electrified needles)
- Nerve reflex testing via electrical pulse

**Body composition/radiation:**
- Total body water determination
- Specific gravity of the whole body
- Total-body radiation count
- Lean body mass calculation (nuclear counter at Los Alamos)

**Other:**
- Lower bowel examination using device nicknamed "Steel Eel"
- Scalps and chests "plastered with electrodes"

### Phase IV -- Aeromedical Laboratory, Wright Air Development Center, Wright-Patterson AFB, Dayton, OH (~1 week per group; "six days and three nights")

**Cardiovascular stress tests (7 tests identified):**
1. Flack test (expel breath to keep ball atop mercury column)
2. Valsalva overshoot test (Flack test + blood pressure measurement)
3. Partial Pressure Suit test (psychological + cardiovascular tests in simulated high-altitude environment)
4. Tilt Table test (cardiovascular recovery after exercise on angled platform; 25 minutes inclined)
5. Harvard Step test (step 20 inches to platform every 2 seconds for 5 minutes)
6. Cold Pressor test (feet in ice water at 4C/39.2F for up to 7 minutes; BP and HR measured each minute)
7. Treadmill test (walked until heart rate reached 180 bpm per History.com)

**Environmental stress tests:**
- Pressure chamber (simulated altitude of 65,000 feet for 1 hour)
- Heat chamber (130 degrees Fahrenheit for 2 hours)
- Noise exposure (math problems in 145-decibel rooms)
- Sensory deprivation/isolation chamber (dark, soundproofed; duration unspecified; wore EEG leads)
- Acceleration/centrifuge tests
- Vibration tests
- Balloon inflation to exhaustion

**Anthropometric measurements:**
- Full body measurements (details not enumerated in sources found)

**Psychological tests (13 for personality/motivation):**
- Rorschach Inkblot Test
- Thematic Apperception Test (TAT)
- Minnesota Multiphasic Personality Inventory (MMPI)
- "Who Am I?" Twenty Statements Test
- (Remaining 9 personality tests not individually named in sources found)

**Intellectual/aptitude tests (12):**
- 12 intelligence tests administered (specific names not enumerated beyond the three Voas tests given in Washington)
- Cognitive function tests performed under stress (noise, heat, discomfort)

**Psychiatric evaluation (~30 hours total):**
- Continuous psychiatric interviews (Ruff and Levy)
- Candidates lived with two psychologists throughout the week
- Candidates were required to "sell" themselves to examiners
- Standardized military aptitude exams for officer candidates and aviators

6. **Donald K. "Deke" Slayton with Michael Cassutt, *Deke! U.S. Manned Space: From Mercury to the Shuttle* (New York: Forge, 1994)** -- Contains selection account; Slayton was tested at Lovelace alongside Carpenter and Jim Lovell.

7. **M. Scott Carpenter et al., *We Seven: By the Astronauts Themselves* (New York: Simon & Schuster, 1962)** -- First Mercury astronaut memoir. Contains collective firsthand account of the selection tests.

8. **Tom Wolfe, *The Right Stuff* (New York: Farrar, Straus and Giroux, 1979)** -- Chapters 5-6 contain the most culturally famous account of the Lovelace/Wright-Patterson tests. Literary nonfiction rather than technical documentation, but contains vivid descriptions of specific procedures. The "enema rebellion" passage is widely referenced.

9. **Colin Burgess, *Selecting the Mercury Seven* (Springer, 2011)** -- Chapters 6-7 ("Lovelace Clinic, New Mexico" and "Wright-Patterson: The Final Hurdle") are the most focused published treatment. Paywalled on Springer; partially available on vdoc.pub and epdf.pub.

10. **Matthew H. Hersch, *Inventing the American Astronaut* (Palgrave Macmillan, 2012)** -- Chapter 2 ("Project Astronaut") contains detailed analysis of the selection process and Ruff/Levy's work.
